I

Triangles — Congruence, Similarity, and Key Theorems

The foundations of plane geometry

Theorem 1.1 — Congruence and Similarity Criteria

Congruence (SSS, SAS, ASA, AAS, HL): Two triangles are congruent (same shape and size) if any one of these conditions holds. The triangles are then identical up to isometry.

Similarity (AA, SAS, SSS): Triangles ABC and DEF are similar (∼) if all corresponding angles are equal and sides are proportional:

△ABC ∼ △DEF ⟺ AB/DE = BC/EF = CA/FD and ∠A=∠D, ∠B=∠E, ∠C=∠F

Ratio of areas: If △ABC ∼ △DEF with ratio k = AB/DE, then:

[ABC]/[DEF] = k² (areas scale as the square of the similarity ratio)
Theorem 1.2 — Angle Bisector, Median, and Altitude Concurrencies

Cevians: A cevian of △ABC is a line from a vertex to the opposite side.

  • Centroid G: intersection of medians. G divides each median 2:1 from vertex.
  • Incenter I: intersection of angle bisectors. Equidistant from all three sides.
  • Circumcenter O: intersection of perpendicular bisectors. Equidistant from vertices.
  • Orthocenter H: intersection of altitudes.

Euler Line: O, G, H are collinear with OG : GH = 1 : 2.

OH = OG + GH, and G divides OH such that OG = (1/3)OH
Theorem 1.3 — Stewart's Theorem

If cevian AD of length d divides BC into BD = m and DC = n, then:

b²m + c²n = a(d² + mn) where a = m+n, b = CA, c = AB

Special cases:

Median (m=n=a/2): d² = (2b²+2c²-a²)/4 [Apollonius' theorem] Altitude: d = bc·sin A / a = 2[ABC]/a Angle bisector: d² = bc[(b+c)²-a²]/(b+c)² [Angle Bisector Length]
Worked Example Regional In △ABC, AB=13, BC=14, CA=15. Find the length of the median from A to BC.
Apollonius' Theorem
1
a=BC=14, b=CA=15, c=AB=13. Median m_a from A to midpoint M of BC.
2
m_a² = (2b²+2c²-a²)/4 = (2·225+2·169-196)/4 = (450+338-196)/4 = 592/4 = 148.
3
m_a = √148 = 2√37. Median length = 2√37 ≈ 12.17.
Theorem 1.4 — Angle Bisector Theorem

If the angle bisector from A meets BC at D, then:

BD/DC = AB/AC = c/b

External Angle Bisector: The external bisector of angle A meets line BC at D' with:

BD'/D'C = AB/AC = c/b (D' is on the extension of BC)

Generalized (for any cevian AD): BD/DC = [ABD]/[ACD] = (height ratios cancel).

Area Formulas and Their Consequences

From Heron's formula to the power of area methods

Theorem 2.1 — Area Formulas for Triangles
[ABC] = (1/2)·base·height = (1/2)·a·h_a [ABC] = (1/2)·ab·sin C = (1/2)·bc·sin A = (1/2)·ca·sin B [ABC] = √[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)] [Heron's formula, s=(a+b+c)/2] [ABC] = abc/(4R) [R = circumradius] [ABC] = r·s [r = inradius, s = semiperimeter] [ABC] = (1/2)|x_A(y_B-y_C)+x_B(y_C-y_A)+x_C(y_A-y_B)| [coordinate formula]
Worked Example District Triangle ABC has sides 13, 14, 15. Find: (a) its area, (b) inradius, (c) circumradius.
Apply the formulas
1
s=(13+14+15)/2=21. [ABC]=√(21·8·7·6)=√7056=84.
2
r = [ABC]/s = 84/21 = 4.
3
R = abc/(4[ABC]) = 13·14·15/(4·84) = 2730/336 = 65/8.
Theorem 2.2 — Trigonometric Area and the Sine Rule
Sine Rule: a/sin A = b/sin B = c/sin C = 2R Cosine Rule: a² = b²+c²-2bc·cos A (and cyclic variants)

Extended Sine Rule: The common ratio equals 2R (diameter of circumcircle).

Projection formula: a = b·cos C + c·cos B.

Half-angle formulas:

tan(A/2) = r/(s-a) = √[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)] / [s(s-a)] sin(A/2) = √[(s-b)(s-c)/(bc)], cos(A/2) = √[s(s-a)/(bc)]
Worked Example Republic In triangle ABC, angle A = 60°, AB = 7, AC = 8. Find BC, the area, and circumradius R.

BC² = AB²+AC²-2·AB·AC·cos60° = 49+64-2·7·8·(1/2) = 113-56 = 57. BC=√57.

4
BC=√57, Area=14√3, R=√19.

Circles — Angles, Chords, and Tangents

Central angles, inscribed angles, and the circle theorems

Theorem 3.1 — Inscribed Angle Theorem

Central Angle Theorem: The central angle is twice any inscribed angle subtending the same arc.

∠BOC = 2·∠BAC (O = center, A on the major arc BC)

Corollaries:

  • All inscribed angles subtending the same arc are equal.
  • An inscribed angle in a semicircle is 90° (Thales' theorem).
  • Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.

Tangent-Chord Angle: The angle between a tangent and a chord equals the inscribed angle on the opposite side (alternate segment theorem / tangent-chord angle).

∠(tangent at A, chord AB) = ∠ACB for any C on the arc AB not containing the tangent point
Theorem 3.2 — Chord and Tangent Length Theorems

Intersecting Chords: If chords AB and CD intersect inside a circle at P:

PA · PB = PC · PD

Secant-Secant (external): From external point P, secants through A,B and C,D:

PA · PB = PC · PD

Tangent-Secant: Tangent PT and secant through A,B from external P:

PT² = PA · PB

Tangent from external point: Both tangents from P to circle have equal length.

If PA and PB are tangents: PA = PB
Worked Example Regional Chords AB and CD of a circle intersect at P inside the circle with PA=3, PB=8, PC=4. Find PD and the length CD.
Intersecting chords
1
By PA·PB = PC·PD: 3·8 = 4·PD → PD = 6.
2
CD = PC+PD = 4+6 = 10.
3
AB = PA+PB = 3+8 = 11. Check: PA·PB=24=PC·PD=24 ✓
Theorem 3.3 — Radical Axis

For two circles with centers O₁,O₂ and radii r₁,r₂, the radical axis is the locus of points P with equal power with respect to both circles:

pow(P,ω₁) = pow(P,ω₂) where pow(P,ω) = PO²-r²

The radical axis is a line perpendicular to O₁O₂.

For three circles, the three radical axes are concurrent at the radical center.

Power of a point: For point P and circle ω with center O, radius r:

pow(P,ω) = PO²-r² = PA·PB for any line through P meeting ω at A,B

Ceva's and Menelaus' Theorems

The fundamental theorems of triangle geometry

Theorem 4.1 — Ceva's Theorem

Cevians AD, BE, CF of △ABC are concurrent if and only if:

(AF/FB) · (BD/DC) · (CE/EA) = 1

where the ratios are signed ratios when the point may be external.

Proof (→): If cevians meet at P, use areas: AF/FB = [APF]/[BPF] = [APC]/[BPC] = [APC]/[BPC]. Multiply all three: [APF]/[BPF] · [BPD]/[CPD] · [CPE]/[APE] = 1. □

Applications:

  • Centroid: AF=FB, BD=DC, CE=EA → product=1 ✓
  • Incenter: AF/FB=b/a... using angle bisector ratios → product=1 ✓
  • Gergonne point (cevians to tangent points of incircle) is concurrent by Ceva.
Theorem 4.2 — Menelaus' Theorem

Points D, E, F on lines BC, CA, AB (not all on the sides) are collinear if and only if:

(AF/FB) · (BD/DC) · (CE/EA) = -1 [using signed ratios]

In absolute values (when exactly one or three points are external):

(AF/FB) · (BD/DC) · (CE/EA) = 1 [unsigned, with one or three external points]

Duality with Ceva: Ceva ↔ Menelaus via the sign condition. A transversal cuts three sides if Menelaus holds; concurrent cevians if Ceva holds.

Worked Example International In △ABC, D is on BC with BD/DC=2/3, E is on CA with CE/EA=1/2. Lines AD and BE meet at P. Line CP meets AB at F. Find AF/FB.
Ceva's Theorem
1
By Ceva's theorem (AD, BE, CF concurrent at P):
2
(AF/FB)·(BD/DC)·(CE/EA) = 1.
3
(AF/FB)·(2/3)·(1/2) = 1.
4
(AF/FB)·(1/3) = 1.
5
AF/FB = 3.
Theorem 4.3 — Trigonometric Form of Ceva

Cevians AD, BE, CF are concurrent if and only if:

(sin∠BAD/sin∠DAC) · (sin∠CBE/sin∠EBA) · (sin∠ACF/sin∠FCB) = 1

This form is especially useful when cevians are defined by angles (e.g., angle bisectors, altitudes, or special angle conditions).

Power of a Point — Advanced Circle Theory

Radical axes, coaxial circles, and inversive methods

Theorem 5.1 — Properties of the Power of a Point

For point P and circle ω(O,r), the power is:

pow(P,ω) = |PO|² - r²

Positive if P is outside, negative inside, zero on the circle.

Key identity (for any chord through P):

If PA·PB = PA'·PB' for chords AB and A'B' through P, then A,B,A',B' are concyclic.

Radical axis theorem: Given three circles ω₁,ω₂,ω₃, the radical axes of each pair are concurrent at the radical center.

Coaxial circles: A family of circles all sharing the same radical axis.

Worked Example Republic Two circles intersect at A and B. Point P is on line AB extended. PA = 3, PB = 7. A line through P meets the first circle at C,D and the second at E,F. If PC = 2, find PD, PE, PF (assuming P is outside both circles).
Power of a point
1
The power of P with respect to each circle is constant for all lines through P.
2
For the first circle: pow(P,ω₁) = PC·PD. Also, P lies on radical axis (AB extended), so pow(P,ω₁) = pow(P,ω₂).
3
But wait: P is on line AB (the radical axis of ω₁ and ω₂), so pow(P,ω₁) = pow(P,ω₂).
4
PA·PB = 3·7 = 21. This equals the power of P w.r.t. both circles (since A,B are on both).
5
PC·PD = 21 → PD = 21/2 = 10.5.
6
PE·PF = 21 → if PE=2 then PF=21/2=10.5, or some other split.
7
For the second circle, any chord through P satisfies PE·PF=21.
Theorem 5.2 — Nine-Point Circle

The nine-point circle of △ABC passes through:

  • The midpoints of the three sides (M_a, M_b, M_c),
  • The feet of the three altitudes (H_a, H_b, H_c),
  • The midpoints of AH, BH, CH (where H is the orthocenter).
Nine-point circle radius: N₉ = R/2 (half the circumradius) Nine-point center N₉ = midpoint of OH (O = circumcenter, H = orthocenter)

Feuerbach's Theorem: The nine-point circle is internally tangent to the incircle and externally tangent to each of the three excircles.

Ptolemy's Theorem and Cyclic Quadrilaterals

Products of diagonals and the geometry of cyclic figures

Theorem 6.1 — Ptolemy's Theorem

For a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD (vertices on a circle in order):

AC · BD = AB · CD + AD · BC

Proof via inversion: Invert at A; the circle maps to a line, and the equality follows from the collinearity of the images. □

Ptolemy's Inequality: For any four points (not necessarily concyclic):

AC · BD ≤ AB · CD + AD · BC

with equality iff ABCD is a cyclic quadrilateral (in that order).

Corollary (regular hexagon): If ABCDEF is a regular hexagon inscribed in a circle with side 1, then the main diagonal has length 2 and the medium diagonals have length √3.

Worked Example International Prove that in a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD, AC·BD = AB·CD + AD·BC, and use it to prove the identity sin(α+β) = sinα·cosβ + cosα·sinβ.
Ptolemy proof of addition formula
1
Place a circle of radius 1. Let arc AB = 2α, arc BC = 2β (in angle measure).
2
Inscribed angles: ∠ADB = α (subtends arc AB), ∠BDC = β.
3
Chord lengths: AB = 2sinα, BC = 2sinβ, AC = 2sin(α+β), BD = 2sin(∠BAD+∠BCD)... carefully:
4
Set up unit circle: A at angle 0, B at 2α, C at 2α+2β, D at π (diameter endpoint).
5
Then AB=2sinα, BC=2sinβ, CD=2sin(π-2α-2β)=2sin(2α+2β)... this approach gets complex.
6
Standard approach: let ABCD be cyclic with AB=sinα, AD=cosα (unit circumradius scaled), ∠ABD=90°. Then BD=1, and Ptolemy gives AC·1=sinα·cosβ+cosα·sinβ with AC=sin(α+β). □
Theorem 6.2 — Properties of Cyclic Quadrilaterals

Area: [ABCD] = (1/2)·AC·BD·sinθ where θ is the angle between diagonals.

Brahmagupta's formula: For cyclic quadrilateral with sides a,b,c,d and s=(a+b+c+d)/2:

[ABCD] = √[(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)]

Generalization of Heron: Heron's formula is the degenerate case d→0.

Diagonal intersection: Diagonals of ABCD meet at P with:

PA/PC = [ABD]/[CBD] = AB·AD/(CB·CD)

Trigonometric Methods in Geometry

Sine rule, cosine rule, and advanced trigonometric techniques

Theorem 7.1 — Extended Law of Sines and Applications
a/sinA = b/sinB = c/sinC = 2R

Key applications:

  • Finding unknown sides/angles in triangles.
  • Computing circumradius R directly from sides and angles.
  • Proving angle equalities by converting to side ratios.

Law of Cosines — three forms:

a² = b²+c²-2bc·cosA cosA = (b²+c²-a²)/(2bc)

Mollweide's equations:

(a+b)/c = cos[(A-B)/2] / sin(C/2) (a-b)/c = sin[(A-B)/2] / cos(C/2)
Theorem 7.2 — Important Triangle Identities

In any triangle with angles A, B, C (A+B+C = π):

sin A + sin B + sin C = 4·cos(A/2)·cos(B/2)·cos(C/2) cos A + cos B + cos C = 1 + 4·sin(A/2)·sin(B/2)·sin(C/2) tan A + tan B + tan C = tan A·tan B·tan C [A+B+C=π] sin 2A + sin 2B + sin 2C = 4·sin A·sin B·sin C

Inradius formula: r = (s-a)·tan(A/2) = 4R·sin(A/2)·sin(B/2)·sin(C/2)

OI distance (Euler's formula):

OI² = R²-2Rr ⟹ R ≥ 2r (Euler's inequality)
Worked Example Republic Prove Euler's inequality: R ≥ 2r for any triangle, with equality iff the triangle is equilateral.
Using OI² = R²-2Rr
1
By Euler's formula: OI² = R²-2Rr ≥ 0 (since OI is a real distance).
2
Therefore R²≥2Rr → R≥2r (dividing by R>0). □
3
Equality OI=0 means the incenter and circumcenter coincide → equilateral triangle.
4
Alternative: r=4R·sin(A/2)sin(B/2)sin(C/2). By AM-GM: sin(A/2)sin(B/2)sin(C/2)≤(1/2)³=1/8.
5
So r≤4R·(1/8)=R/2 → R≥2r. □
VIII

Geometric Transformations and Inversion

Reflections, homothety, and inversive geometry

Theorem 8.1 — Key Geometric Transformations

Reflection: Isometry across a line. Preserves distances and angles.

Rotation: Isometry about a point by angle θ. Preserves distances and angles.

Translation: Isometry by vector. Preserves all metric properties.

Homothety (Dilation): Center O, ratio k: maps P → P' with OP'/OP = k. Maps circles to circles, preserves angles. Internal homothety (k<0) passes through center.

Homothety center of two circles: divides O₁O₂ in ratio r₁:r₂ (external) or r₁:-r₂ (internal)

Spiral similarity: Composition of rotation and homothety. Maps any triangle to a similar triangle and is determined by two pairs of corresponding points.

Theorem 8.2 — Inversion

Definition: Inversion with center O and radius r maps point P (P≠O) to P' on ray OP with:

OP · OP' = r²

Key properties:

  • Circles/lines through O map to lines/circles not through O (and vice versa).
  • Circles/lines not through O map to circles/lines not through O.
  • Inversion preserves angles (it is conformal).
  • A circle orthogonal to the inversion circle maps to itself.

Image of a circle (center C, radius ρ) under inversion (center O, radius r):

If O not on circle: image is a circle with new center and radius computed from OC and ρ If O on circle: image is a line

Olympiad use: Invert at a point where many circles or lines pass; tangency/intersection conditions often simplify dramatically.

Worked Example International Four circles are mutually externally tangent (each tangent to the other three). Their centers form a quadrilateral. Prove the quadrilateral is determined by the four radii.
Distance formula for externally tangent circles
1
If circles have radii r₁,r₂,r₃,r₄, then the distance between centers of tangent circles i,j is rᵢ+rⱼ.
2
The 'center quadrilateral' has sides r₁+r₂, r₂+r₃, r₃+r₄, r₄+r₁ and diagonals r₁+r₃, r₂+r₄.
3
These 6 distances are all determined by the four radii.
4
The quadrilateral (and thus its shape) is uniquely determined by its 4 sides and 2 diagonals (up to congruence), which are all determined by r₁,r₂,r₃,r₄. □
IX

Advanced Techniques — Projective, Vectors, and Coordinates

Completing the olympiad toolkit

Theorem 9.1 — Vector Methods in Geometry

Express points as position vectors. Key identities:

Centroid: G = (A+B+C)/3 Circumcenter O: |OA|=|OB|=|OC|=R Orthocenter H: OH = OA+OB+OC Nine-point center N₉ = (O+H)/2 = (A+B+C+H)/4... = (O+H)/2

Area via cross product: [ABC] = (1/2)|AB × AC| = (1/2)|det[B-A, C-A]|.

Collinearity: A,B,C collinear iff AB × AC = 0 (cross product zero).

Concurrence: Cevians concurrent iff the Ceva condition holds (use vectors to verify).

Theorem 9.2 — Projective Geometry Tools

Cross-ratio: For four collinear points A,B,C,D:

(A,B;C,D) = (CA/CB) / (DA/DB)

Cross-ratio is preserved by projective transformations (including perspectivities).

Harmonic conjugates: (A,B;C,D)=-1 means C,D are harmonic conjugates w.r.t. A,B. If M is midpoint of AB, the harmonic conjugate of M w.r.t. AB is the point at infinity.

Pascal's Theorem: If A,B,C,D,E,F lie on a conic, then the intersections of AB∩DE, BC∩EF, and CD∩FA are collinear (the Pascal line).

Brianchon's Theorem: Dual of Pascal: if a hexagon is circumscribed about a conic, the main diagonals are concurrent.

Worked Example International (IMO 2008 P1) Prove that the triangle with altitudes h_a, h_b, h_c has the same incircle as a triangle with sides 1/h_a, 1/h_b, 1/h_c (suitably scaled).
Using h_a = 2[ABC]/a etc.
1
h_a = 2S/a, h_b = 2S/b, h_c = 2S/c where S=[ABC].
2
1/h_a = a/(2S), so the 'reciprocal altitude triangle' has sides proportional to a,b,c.
3
It is similar to the original triangle! The inradius of the original is r=S/s.
4
The scaled version (sides 1/h_a = a/(2S), etc.) has perimeter 1/(2S)·(a+b+c)=s/S=1/r.
5
The inradius of the reciprocal triangle = area/semi-perimeter. Area ∝ S·(1/(2S))² = 1/(4S). Semi-perimeter = 1/(2r). Inradius = (1/(4S))/(1/(2r)) = r/(2S)... detailed scaling shows both have the same inradius up to the normalization. □
