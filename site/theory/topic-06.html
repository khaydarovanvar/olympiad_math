AM-GM and Basic Inequalities

The arithmetic-geometric mean inequality and its consequences

Theorem 1.1 — AM-GM Inequality

For non-negative reals a₁,a₂,...,aₙ:

(a₁+a₂+...+aₙ)/n ≥ (a₁·a₂·...·aₙ)^(1/n)

with equality if and only if a₁=a₂=...=aₙ.

Proof (n=2): (a−b)²≥0 ⟹ a²+b²≥2ab ⟹ (a+b)/2≥√(ab). □

Proof (general, by Cauchy's forward-backward induction):
Forward: true for n=2^k by applying n=2 case repeatedly.
Backward: if true for n+1, multiply a₁...aₙ and (a₁+...+aₙ)/n to get true for n. □

Weighted AM-GM: For weights w₁,...,wₙ≥0 summing to 1:

w₁a₁+w₂a₂+...+wₙaₙ ≥ a₁^w₁ · a₂^w₂ · ... · aₙ^wₙ
Worked Example District Prove that for positive reals a,b,c with abc=1: a+b+c≥3.
AM-GM direct
1
By AM-GM: (a+b+c)/3 ≥ (abc)^(1/3) = 1^(1/3) = 1.
2
Therefore a+b+c ≥ 3, with equality when a=b=c=1. □
Worked Example Regional Find the maximum value of x²y³z⁴ subject to x+y+z=1, x,y,z>0.
Weighted AM-GM
1
We want to maximize x²y³z⁴. Weights: 2/9, 3/9, 4/9 (sum=1).
2
Weighted AM-GM: (2/9)·x/(2/9) + (3/9)·y/(3/9) + (4/9)·z/(4/9) ≥ (x/(2/9))^(2/9)·(y/(3/9))^(3/9)·(z/(4/9))^(4/9).
3
Better: by AM-GM applied to 2 copies of x/2, 3 of y/3, 4 of z/4:
4
(x/2+x/2+y/3+y/3+y/3+z/4+z/4+z/4+z/4)/9 ≥ (x/2)²(y/3)³(z/4)⁴)^(1/9).
5
LHS = (x+y+z)/9 = 1/9. So (x/2)²(y/3)³(z/4)⁴ ≤ (1/9)⁹.
6
x²y³z⁴ = (x/2)²·(y/3)³·(z/4)⁴ · 4·27·256 = 2764·(1/9)⁹.
7
Equality: x/2=y/3=z/4 and x+y+z=1 → x=2/9,y=3/9,z=4/9.
8
Max x²y³z⁴ = (2/9)²(3/9)³(4/9)⁴ = 4·27·256/9⁹ = 27648/9⁹.
Theorem 1.2 — Key Consequences of AM-GM
a²+b² ≥ 2ab (a-b)²≥0 a/b + b/a ≥ 2 for a,b>0 (a+b)(1/a+1/b) ≥ 4 Cauchy special case a³+b³+c³ ≥ 3abc (equivalent to AM-GM) a^n+b^n ≥ a^(n-1)b + ab^(n-1) for n≥1 [SOS]

Nesbitt's Inequality: For positive a,b,c:

a/(b+c) + b/(a+c) + c/(a+b) ≥ 3/2
Worked Example District For positive reals a,b,c, prove (a+b+c)(1/a+1/b+1/c) ≥ 9.
Expand + a/b+b/a≥2
1
Expand the product: (a+b+c)(1/a+1/b+1/c) = 3 + (a/b+b/a) + (b/c+c/b) + (a/c+c/a).
2
By AM-GM each paired term satisfies x/y+y/x ≥ 2, so the three pairs contribute at least 6.
3
Therefore the product ≥ 3+6 = 9, with equality iff a=b=c. □
4
This is the AM-HM inequality in disguise: it shows AM ≥ HM for three terms.
Worked Example Regional For non-negative reals a,b,c, prove a³+b³+c³ ≥ 3abc.
Factoring identity (SOS)
1
Use the identity a³+b³+c³−3abc = (a+b+c)(a²+b²+c²−ab−bc−ca).
2
The first factor a+b+c ≥ 0 since the variables are non-negative.
3
The second factor equals ½[(a−b)²+(b−c)²+(c−a)²] ≥ 0.
4
A product of two non-negative quantities is non-negative, so a³+b³+c³ ≥ 3abc. Equality iff a=b=c. □
II

Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

The most powerful elementary inequality

Theorem 2.1 — Cauchy-Schwarz Inequality

For real numbers aᵢ, bᵢ:

(a₁²+a₂²+...+aₙ²)(b₁²+b₂²+...+bₙ²) ≥ (a₁b₁+a₂b₂+...+aₙbₙ)²

Proof: Consider f(t)=∑(aᵢt+bᵢ)²=At²+2Bt+C≥0 for all t. Discriminant ≤0: (2B)²-4AC≤0, i.e. B²≤AC, which is exactly Cauchy-Schwarz. □

Engel form (Titu's Lemma):

a₁²/b₁ + a₂²/b₂ + ... + aₙ²/bₙ ≥ (a₁+a₂+...+aₙ)²/(b₁+b₂+...+bₙ) for bᵢ>0

Equality condition: a₁/b₁ = a₂/b₂ = ... = aₙ/bₙ (vectors are proportional).

Worked Example Regional Prove that for positive reals a,b,c: (a+b+c)² ≤ 3(a²+b²+c²).
Cauchy-Schwarz
1
By Cauchy-Schwarz with vectors (a,b,c) and (1,1,1):
2
(a·1+b·1+c·1)² ≤ (a²+b²+c²)(1²+1²+1²) = 3(a²+b²+c²). □
3
Equality when a=b=c.
Worked Example International Prove Nesbitt's inequality: a/(b+c)+b/(a+c)+c/(a+b) ≥ 3/2 for positive a,b,c.
Three methods
1
Method 1 (add 1 to each): Each term +1: (a+b+c)/(b+c)+(a+b+c)/(a+c)+(a+b+c)/(a+b) ≥ 9 by AM-HM (Cauchy). Divide by (a+b+c) to... actually: sum of 3 terms each = (a+b+c)/denominator. By Cauchy: ∑(a+b+c)/(b+c) ≥ 9·(a+b+c)/[2(a+b+c)] = 9/2. Divide by 1: original sum = above − 3 ≥ 9/2−3=3/2. □
2
Method 2 (SOS): LHS−3/2 = [a(a+b)(a+c)+b(b+c)(a+b)+c(a+c)(b+c)−(3/2)(a+b)(b+c)(a+c)] / [(a+b)(b+c)(a+c)]. Numerator = sum of squares after expansion ≥ 0.
3
Method 3 (Titu): By Titu: a/(b+c)=a²/(ab+ac). Sum ≥ (a+b+c)²/[2(ab+bc+ca)] ≥ 3/2 since (a+b+c)²≥3(ab+bc+ca). □
Worked Example Republic For positive reals a,b,c with a+b+c=1, find the minimum of a/(b+c²) + b/(c+a²) + c/(a+b²).
Titu's Lemma
1
With a+b+c=1: b+c=1-a, so b+c² = b+c·c. By Titu or direct estimate with a+b+c=1:
2
b+c²=b+c²≤b+c (since c≤1). Hard exact approach: check equality at a=b=c=1/3.
3
At a=b=c=1/3: each term = (1/3)/((1/3)+(1/9))=(1/3)/(4/9)=3/4. Sum=9/4.
4
Show this is the minimum using SOS or Lagrange multipliers. Minimum = 9/4 at a=b=c=1/3.

Power Mean and Jensen's Inequality

Convexity and the hierarchy of means

Theorem 3.1 — Power Mean Inequality

For positive reals x₁,...,xₙ and r≠0, the power mean of order r is:

M_r = ((x₁^r+x₂^r+...+xₙ^r)/n)^(1/r)

For r=0: M₀=geometric mean; r=∞: M_∞=max; r=−∞: M_(-∞)=min.

M_(-∞) ≤ ... ≤ M_(-1) ≤ M_0 ≤ M_1 ≤ M_2 ≤ ... ≤ M_∞

i.e., M_r is non-decreasing in r. In particular: HM ≤ GM ≤ AM ≤ QM.

HM = n/(1/x₁+...+1/xₙ) ≤ GM = (x₁...xₙ)^(1/n) ≤ AM = (x₁+...+xₙ)/n ≤ QM = √((∑xᵢ²)/n)
Worked Example Regional For positive reals a,b,c with a+b+c=1, prove a²+b²+c² ≥ 1/3 and 1/a+1/b+1/c ≥ 9.
QM-AM and AM-HM
1
First part (QM ≥ AM): √((a²+b²+c²)/3) ≥ (a+b+c)/3 = 1/3.
2
Squaring: (a²+b²+c²)/3 ≥ 1/9, so a²+b²+c² ≥ 1/3.
3
Second part (AM ≥ HM): (a+b+c)/3 ≥ 3/(1/a+1/b+1/c).
4
Rearranging: 1/a+1/b+1/c ≥ 9/(a+b+c) = 9. Both have equality at a=b=c=1/3. □
Worked Example International For positive reals a,b,c, prove ∛((a³+b³+c³)/3) ≥ (a+b+c)/3.
This is the Power Mean inequality M₃ ≥ M₁.
Power Mean (order 3 vs order 1)
1
The left side is M₃ = ((a³+b³+c³)/3)^(1/3); the right side is M₁ = (a+b+c)/3. Power Mean gives M₃ ≥ M₁.
2
Equivalently, cubing both sides: a³+b³+c³ ≥ (a+b+c)³/9.
3
Direct check: f(x)=x³ is convex, so by Jensen f((a+b+c)/3) ≤ (f(a)+f(b)+f(c))/3, i.e. ((a+b+c)/3)³ ≤ (a³+b³+c³)/3.
4
Taking cube roots gives the claim, with equality iff a=b=c. □
Theorem 3.2 — Jensen's Inequality

If f is a convex function (f''≥0) and λ₁,...,λₙ≥0 with ∑λᵢ=1:

f(λ₁x₁+λ₂x₂+...+λₙxₙ) ≤ λ₁f(x₁)+λ₂f(x₂)+...+λₙf(xₙ)

For concave f, the inequality reverses.

Common convex functions: x², eˣ, x log x (x>0), 1/x (x>0).

Common concave functions: log x, √x, sin x (on [0,π]).

Proof: By induction using the 2-variable convexity condition f(λx+(1-λ)y)≤λf(x)+(1-λ)f(y). □

Worked Example Regional Let a,b,c>0 with a+b+c=3. Prove a²+b²+c²≥3 and find when equality holds.
Jensen / QM-AM
1
f(x)=x² is convex. By Jensen: f((a+b+c)/3) ≤ (f(a)+f(b)+f(c))/3.
2
((a+b+c)/3)² ≤ (a²+b²+c²)/3 → 9/9 ≤ (a²+b²+c²)/3 → a²+b²+c²≥3. □
3
Equality: a=b=c=1.
Worked Example International For positive reals with a+b+c=1, prove: a^a·b^b·c^c ≥ (1/3)^(a+b+c) = 1/3.
Jensen on log
1
Take log: a·log a+b·log b+c·log c ≥ (a+b+c)·log(1/3)=-log 3.
2
f(x)=x log x is convex (f''=1/x>0). By Jensen: f(a)+f(b)+f(c) ≥ 3f((a+b+c)/3)=3·(1/3)log(1/3)=−log 3. □
3
So a^a·b^b·c^c ≥ e^(−log 3) = 1/3. Equality when a=b=c=1/3.

Rearrangement and Chebyshev

Order matters: when similar sequences help

Theorem 4.1 — Rearrangement Inequality

If a₁≤a₂≤...≤aₙ and b₁≤b₂≤...≤bₙ are real sequences, then for any permutation σ:

∑aᵢb_(n+1-i) ≤ ∑aᵢb_σ(i) ≤ ∑aᵢbᵢ

i.e., the sum ∑aᵢbᵢ is maximized when both sequences are sorted in the same order (both increasing) and minimized when sorted in opposite orders.

Proof sketch: Any non-sorted arrangement can be improved by a transposition that sorts it further. Iterate to get the maximum arrangement. □

Worked Example Regional For positive reals a,b,c, prove a²+b²+c² ≥ ab+bc+ca using rearrangement.
Same sequence vs a permutation
1
Take the two identical sequences (a,b,c) and (a,b,c), sorted the same way.
2
The "sorted with itself" pairing gives the maximum sum: a·a+b·b+c·c = a²+b²+c².
3
Any other pairing is smaller. Pairing (a,b,c) with the shifted (b,c,a) gives ab+bc+ca.
4
By the Rearrangement Inequality, a²+b²+c² ≥ ab+bc+ca, with equality iff a=b=c. □
Worked Example Republic For positive reals a,b,c, prove a/b + b/c + c/a ≥ 3.
Rearrangement / AM-GM
1
Consider the sequences (a,b,c) and (1/a,1/b,1/c). The oppositely-sorted pairing gives the minimum: a·(1/a)+b·(1/b)+c·(1/c) = 3.
2
A cyclic pairing gives a·(1/b)+b·(1/c)+c·(1/a) = a/b+b/c+c/a, which is at least the minimum.
3
Hence a/b+b/c+c/a ≥ 3. (Equivalently, by AM-GM: a/b+b/c+c/a ≥ 3∛(a/b·b/c·c/a) = 3.) Equality iff a=b=c. □
Theorem 4.2 — Chebyshev's Sum Inequality

If a₁≤...≤aₙ and b₁≤...≤bₙ (both sorted the same way):

n·∑aᵢbᵢ ≥ (∑aᵢ)(∑bᵢ)

If one sequence is increasing and the other decreasing, the inequality reverses.

Proof: Expand n·∑aᵢbᵢ−(∑aᵢ)(∑bᵢ) = ∑_(i<j)(aᵢ−aⱼ)(bᵢ−bⱼ)≥0 since both differences have the same sign (both sorted same way). □

Worked Example Republic Let a≥b≥c>0. Prove a²/b + b²/c + c²/a ≥ a+b+c.
Rearrangement / Chebyshev
1
Let xᵢ=(a,b,c) and yᵢ=(a/b, b/c, c/a) — not obviously sorted. Use the following:
2
a²/b+b²/c+c²/a = a·(a/b)+b·(b/c)+c·(c/a).
3
By AM-GM: a/b+b/a ≥ 2. But we need a cleaner approach.
4
By AM-GM on each term: a²/b ≥ 2a−b (since a²/b+b≥2a). Sum: ∑a²/b+∑b≥2∑a → ∑a²/b ≥ 2∑a−∑a=∑a. □
Worked Example International Prove: for positive a,b,c with a+b+c=3, we have a/b²+b/c²+c/a² ≥ a+b+c = 3. (Harder: minimize a²/b+b²/c+c²/a subject to a+b+c=3.)
Chebyshev + Cauchy
1
For the harder problem: minimize S=a²/b+b²/c+c²/a.
2
By AM-GM: a²/b+b ≥ 2a → a²/b ≥ 2a-b. Sum: S≥2(a+b+c)-(b+c+a)=a+b+c=3.
3
So S≥3 with equality when a²/b=2a-b → a=b, and similarly b=c. Thus a=b=c=1. Min S=3.

SOS and Schur's Inequality

Sum of Squares and Schur techniques

Theorem 5.1 — SOS Method (Sum of Squares)

To prove f(a,b,c)≥0, write f as a sum of squares (or sum of terms known to be non-negative).

SOS decomposition: f = ∑ λᵢ(aᵢx+bᵢy+cᵢz)² with λᵢ≥0.

pqr method: For symmetric inequalities in a,b,c, set p=a+b+c, q=ab+bc+ca, r=abc. Express the inequality in terms of p,q,r. Use Newton's inequalities: p²≥3q, q²≥3pr.

uvw method: Set u=p/3=(a+b+c)/3, v²=q/3=(ab+bc+ca)/3, w³=r=abc. Then the constraint is u³≥uv²≥w³ (AM-GM chain) and u²≥v² (another AM-GM).

Worked Example Republic For real a,b,c, prove a⁴+b⁴+c⁴ ≥ a²b²+b²c²+c²a² ≥ abc(a+b+c).
A two-step chain, each proven by sum of squares.
SOS twice
1
Left inequality: a⁴+b⁴+c⁴ − (a²b²+b²c²+c²a²) = ½[(a²−b²)²+(b²−c²)²+(c²−a²)²] ≥ 0.
2
Right inequality: apply the identity x²+y²+z² ≥ xy+yz+zx with x=ab, y=bc, z=ca.
3
That gives a²b²+b²c²+c²a² ≥ ab·bc+bc·ca+ca·ab = abc(a+b+c).
4
This middle step is itself ½[(ab−bc)²+(bc−ca)²+(ca−ab)²] ≥ 0. Chaining both, equality iff |a|=|b|=|c|. □
Theorem 5.2 — Schur's Inequality

For non-negative reals a,b,c and t>0:

aᵗ(a-b)(a-c) + bᵗ(b-a)(b-c) + cᵗ(c-a)(c-b) ≥ 0

with equality when a=b=c or when two of a,b,c are equal and the third is 0.

Proof for t=1: WLOG a≥b≥c≥0. Let a=c+s+t, b=c+s (s,t≥0). Expand and simplify; the result is a sum of non-negative terms. □

Key special case (t=2): a²(a-b)(a-c)+b²(b-a)(b-c)+c²(c-a)(c-b)≥0.

Application: For a+b+c=1: Schur with t=1 gives q≤p²/3+r/p... many symmetric ineqs follow.

Worked Example Republic For non-negative a,b,c with a+b+c=1, prove a²+b²+c² ≥ ab+bc+ca.
SOS
1
2(a²+b²+c²)-2(ab+bc+ca) = (a-b)²+(b-c)²+(c-a)² ≥ 0. □
2
Equivalently: (a+b+c)²=a²+b²+c²+2(ab+bc+ca)≥3(ab+bc+ca) gives a²+b²+c²≥ab+bc+ca.
Worked Example International For positive reals a,b,c, prove a³+b³+c³+abc ≥ ab(a+b)+bc(b+c)+ca(c+a).
Schur t=1
1
This is Schur's inequality t=1. LHS-RHS = a(a-b)(a-c)+b(b-a)(b-c)+c(c-a)(c-b)≥0. □
2
Expanding LHS-RHS = a³+b³+c³+abc-a²b-ab²-b²c-bc²-a²c-ac² = Schur sum ≥ 0. □

Muirhead and Symmetric Inequalities

Majorization and the art of mixing variables

Theorem 6.1 — Muirhead's Inequality

For non-negative reals a,b,c and exponent vectors α,β, the symmetric sum is:

[α₁,α₂,α₃] = ∑_sym a^α₁ b^α₂ c^α₃ (sum over all permutations)

Muirhead's Theorem: [α]≥[β] for all positive reals a,b,c if and only if α majorizes β (written α≻β):

α majorizes β iff ∑_{i=1}^{k} α_[i] ≥ ∑_{i=1}^{k} β_[i] for all k, with equality for k=n

Example: [3,0,0]≥[2,1,0]≥[1,1,1] since (3,0,0)≻(2,1,0)≻(1,1,1).

Warning: Muirhead only works for symmetric inequalities with all non-negative reals. For competition use, SOS or direct methods are safer if the condition isn't symmetric.

Worked Example Regional Prove: for positive a,b,c, we have a³+b³+c³ ≥ a²b+b²c+c²a.
Hint: use Muirhead or SOS.
By Muirhead
1
LHS corresponds to [3,0,0]/2 (symmetric sum). RHS to [2,1,0]/2... wait: a²b+b²c+c²a is a cyclic sum, not fully symmetric.
2
Cyclic (not symmetric) → Muirhead doesn't directly apply.
3
Instead: a³+b³+c³-a²b-b²c-c²a = a²(a-b)+c²(c-a)+b²(b-c)+c²(c-b)+b²(b-a)... use rearrangement.
4
By AM-GM: a³+b³ ≥ a²b+ab² ≥ 2a^(3/2)b^(3/2)... not clean.
5
Direct SOS: a³-a²b+b³-b²c+c³-c²a = a²(a-b)-c²(a-c)+b²(b-c). WLOG a≥b≥c: a-b≥0,a-c≥0,b-c≥0, so each term≥0. □
Theorem 6.2 — Mixing Variables Method

SOS-Schur technique: Many 3-variable symmetric inequalities f(a,b,c)≥0 can be proven by: 1. Verify equality case (usually a=b=c or two equal, one zero) 2. Use substitution a=x+y, b=y, c=0 to reduce to 2 variables 3. Show the remaining inequality holds

EV (Equal Variables) method: For symmetric convex/concave functions, the extremum often occurs at a boundary: two variables equal, or at 0.

Normalization: When the inequality is homogeneous, set a+b+c=1 or abc=1 or a+b+c+d=4, etc. to fix the scale.

Worked Example International (USAMO 2001) For non-negative reals a,b,c, prove a²+b²+c²+2abc+1 ≥ 2(ab+bc+ca).
Equality at a=b=c=1. Use a WLOG ordering ("two on the same side of 1") to collapse to an SOS.
Mixing variables → SOS
1
Among a,b,c, at least two lie on the same side of 1, so WLOG (a−1)(b−1) ≥ 0, i.e. ab ≥ a+b−1.
2
Then 2abc ≥ 2c(a+b−1) = 2ac+2bc−2c.
3
Substitute into the left minus right: a²+b²+c²+2abc+1−2ab−2bc−2ca ≥ a²+b²+c²+(2ac+2bc−2c)+1−2ab−2bc−2ca.
4
The 2ac, 2bc, 2ca terms cancel, leaving a²+b²−2ab + c²−2c+1.
5
That is exactly (a−b)² + (c−1)² ≥ 0. So the original inequality holds, with equality at a=b=c=1. □

Trigonometric and Geometric Inequalities

Ineqs in triangles and circles

Theorem 7.1 — Triangle Inequalities

For angles A,B,C of a triangle (A+B+C=π, all positive):

sin A + sin B + sin C ≤ 3√3/2 (max at equilateral) cos A + cos B + cos C ≤ 3/2 (max at equilateral) tan A + tan B + tan C ≥ 3√3 (min at equilateral, acute triangle) sin A · sin B · sin C ≤ 3√3/8 (AM-GM + concavity of sin)

By Jensen (sin is concave on (0,π)): sin A+sin B+sin C ≤ 3 sin((A+B+C)/3)=3 sin(60°)=3√3/2. □

Theorem 7.2 — Ptolemy's Inequality

For any four points A,B,C,D in the plane:

AC · BD ≤ AB · CD + AD · BC

with equality if and only if A,B,C,D are concyclic (in that order on a circle).

Application: In a cyclic quadrilateral ABCD: AC·BD=AB·CD+AD·BC (Ptolemy's theorem).

Erdős-Mordell inequality: For point P inside triangle ABC with distances d_a,d_b,d_c to the sides and R_a,R_b,R_c to the vertices:

R_a + R_b + R_c ≥ 2(d_a + d_b + d_c)
Worked Example International Let ABC be equilateral and P a point on the arc BC of its circumcircle (not containing A). Prove PA = PB + PC.
A clean application of Ptolemy's equality for concyclic points.
Ptolemy on cyclic quadrilateral ABPC
1
Since P lies on the circumcircle, the points A, B, P, C are concyclic, so Ptolemy holds with equality.
2
Apply it to quadrilateral ABPC (diagonals AP and BC): AP·BC = AB·PC + AC·PB.
3
The triangle is equilateral, so AB = AC = BC. Divide through by this common length.
4
This yields PA = PB + PC. □
Worked Example Republic In an acute triangle with sides a,b,c and area S, prove a²+b²+c² ≥ 4S√3.
AM-GM + area formula
1
S=(1/2)ab sin C. Area by formula: S=√[s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)] (Heron).
2
We use: for all angles, sin C ≤ 1, and a²+b²≥2ab, so a²+b²≥2ab≥4S/sin C·sin C=4S·... not clean.
3
Better: use a²+b²+c²≥4S√3. This is the Hadwiger-Finsler inequality: a²+b²+c²≥4S√3+( a-b)²+(b-c)²+(c-a)² ≥4S√3.
4
Proof: use S=(1/4)√((a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c))≤(√3/4)·((a²+b²+c²)/3)... by AM-GM on the factors.
5
Equality holds for the equilateral triangle.

Inequalities with Constraints

Lagrange multipliers, substitutions, and normalization

Theorem 8.1 — Standard Substitutions

Theorem 9.1 — Complete Strategy for Olympiad Inequalities

Step 1 — Identify the type: Symmetric? Cyclic? Homogeneous? Conditional?

Step 2 — Check homogeneity: If degree d is uniform, normalize (set sum=1 or product=1).

Step 3 — Find equality cases: Usually a=b=c=... or boundary points. This guides the proof.

Step 4 — Choose technique:

  • 3 variables, symmetric, degree ≤ 4: try SOS or pqr
  • Known mean chain: AM-GM, Cauchy-Schwarz, Power Mean
  • Triangle: Ravi substitution, trigonometric form
  • Convex function: Jensen's inequality
  • Sorted sequences: Rearrangement, Chebyshev

Step 5 — Write clean proof: State the inequality used, check equality, conclude.

Solution
1
Prove a₁a₂...aₙ ≤ ((a₁+...+aₙ)/n)^n for non-negative aᵢ by Cauchy's forward-backward induction.

3

4
